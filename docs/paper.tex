\begin{abstract}
Recommender systems have evolved from simple neighborhood-based methods to
complex deep learning models that incorporate side information such as text and
images. However, many comparative studies focus on a single dataset and lack
rigorous statistical validation against published baselines. We present
CineRec, a practical study that evaluates six recommendation models of
increasing complexity---UserCF, ItemCF, SVD, NeuMF, LightGCN, and
MultiModalNCF---on two domains: MovieLens 100K and Amazon Video\_Games. We
reproduce two published baselines (BPR-MF and VBPR) under the same
evaluation protocol, report results as mean$\pm$standard deviation over five
random seeds, and conduct both modality ablation and cold-start studies. Our
findings show that (1) the ranking of models is largely consistent across
domains, (2) content features provide measurable gains in cold-start settings
but have limited impact on overall ranking quality, and (3) MultiModalNCF
performs on par with VBPR, a well-established content-aware baseline. We
release the full codebase and evaluation scripts, and provide instructions to
reproduce all datasets from public sources, to support reproducibility.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Recommender systems are a core component of modern information retrieval,
powering personalized content discovery across e-commerce, media, and social
platforms. The field has seen a rapid evolution of methods, from early
collaborative filtering approaches based on user-item interaction matrices to
modern deep learning models that incorporate rich side information such as text
descriptions, images, and knowledge graphs.

Despite this rapid progress, many comparative studies in recommender systems
suffer from three limitations: (1) they are evaluated on a single dataset,
limiting the generalizability of conclusions, (2) they lack comparison against
published baselines under matched conditions, and (3) results are often
reported as single-point estimates without statistical significance testing.

In this work, we present CineRec, a practical study designed to address these
gaps. Our contributions are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We implement and evaluate six recommendation models spanning the full
        evolution from classical neighborhood methods to deep multi-modal
        models, under a unified evaluation protocol.
  \item We reproduce two published baselines---BPR-MF~\cite{rendle2009}
        and VBPR~\cite{he2016vbpr}---on the same split, allowing direct
        comparison against established methods.
  \item We validate all conclusions across two datasets: MovieLens 100K and
        Amazon Video\_Games, testing whether observed patterns generalize
        across domains.
  \item We report all neural model results as mean$\pm$standard deviation over
        five random seeds, distinguishing real effects from seed noise.
  \item We conduct both modality ablation and cold-start studies, quantifying
        the contribution of each content channel.
\end{enumerate}

The study design is summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:overview}. The remainder of
this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related
work. Section 3 describes our methodology and model suite. Section 4 presents
experimental setup and results. Section 5 discusses findings and limitations.
Section 6 concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Neighborhood-Based Collaborative Filtering}
User-based and item-based collaborative filtering~\cite{deshpande2004} are
among the earliest and simplest recommendation approaches. They rely on
similarity measures between users or items to generate recommendations. While
simple, they remain competitive baselines and serve as the foundation for more
complex methods.

\subsection{Matrix Factorization}
Matrix factorization methods such as SVD~\cite{koren2009} and
BPR~\cite{rendle2009} transform the recommendation problem into a latent factor
space. BPR, in particular, introduced a pairwise ranking loss that has become a
standard for implicit feedback recommendation.

\subsection{Neural Collaborative Filtering}
NeuMF~\cite{he2017ncf} combined matrix factorization with multi-layer
perceptrons to model non-linear user-item interactions, demonstrating that deep
learning can improve recommendation quality over traditional matrix
factorization.

\subsection{Graph-Based Recommendation}
LightGCN~\cite{he2020lightgcn} simplified graph convolutional networks for
recommendation by removing feature transformation and non-linear activation,
achieving strong performance with increased efficiency.

\subsection{Multi-Modal Recommendation}
VBPR~\cite{he2016vbpr} extended BPR with visual features, demonstrating that
content information can improve recommendation accuracy, especially for
cold-start items. Multi-modal recommendation has since become an active
research area, incorporating text, images, and other side information.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Model Suite}
We evaluate six models of increasing complexity, plus two published baselines.
Table~\ref{tab:config} summarizes all model configurations; Figure~\ref{fig:model}
details the MultiModalNCF architecture.

Key details:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{NeuMF}: Neural Matrix Factorization combining GMF and MLP
        branches.
  \item \textbf{LightGCN}: Simplified graph convolutional network with BPR loss.
  \item \textbf{MultiModalNCF}: Our multi-modal extension of NeuMF. A content
        tower projects each modality (text 384d, image 2048d, genre 18d) to
        32 dimensions each, concatenates to 96d, then projects to 32d before
        fusing with the behavioural embedding.
  \item \textbf{Positive threshold}: Neural models binarise ratings
        $\geq4$ as positive during training.
  \item \textbf{Negative sampling}: 4 negatives per positive for NeuMF and
        MultiModalNCF; BPR-based models use standard BPR sampling.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
We use the standard leave-last-5-out split: the five most recent interactions of
each user are held out as the test set. All models are evaluated against the
same candidate set (all items minus the user's training interactions). We
report HR@K and NDCG@K at $K\in\{10,20\}$; Recall@10 is additionally plotted in
Figure~\ref{fig:main}.

Neural models and both baselines are trained with five random seeds (42--46)
and reported as mean$\pm$standard deviation. Deterministic models (UserCF,
ItemCF, SVD) have zero spread. Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} summarises the
protocol.

\subsection{Content Features}
We precompute content features shared across all content-aware models:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Text}: Sentence-BERT (all-MiniLM-L6-v2)~\cite{reimers2019}
        embeddings of movie overviews, 384-dimensional.
  \item \textbf{Image}: ResNet-50 (pool5)~\cite{he2016resnet} embeddings of
        movie posters, 2048-dimensional.
  \item \textbf{Genre}: Multi-hot genre vectors, 18-dimensional.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experiments}
All experimental results below are read from the committed result artefacts of
release v1.0.0 (2026-10-13) and are reproducible via the repository's make
targets; the CI figures are as reported by the CI run on v1.0.0.

\subsection{Datasets}
We conduct experiments on two datasets:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{MovieLens 100K}~\cite{harper2015}: 943 users, 1,682 items, 100,000 ratings
        (1--5 scale). Users with $\geq20$ ratings.
  \item \textbf{Amazon Video\_Games}~\cite{hou2024}: 2,288 users, 4,753 items, 75,980 ratings
        (1--5 scale). Heavy users with $\geq20$ ratings.
\end{itemize}

The Amazon slice is built deterministically from the 5-core Amazon Reviews 2023
Video\_Games dataset by keeping users with $\geq20$ ratings and re-applying the
item 5-core rule.
The original 5-core dataset is publicly available from the Amazon Reviews
dataset repository; our slice definition and processing scripts are included in
the codebase.

Poster and overview metadata are retrieved from the TMDB API and remain subject
to TMDB's terms; MovieLens 100K is used under its non-commercial research terms,
and the project's code is released under the MIT licence.

\subsection{Main Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} shows the main model comparison on MovieLens 100K. Key
observations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The expected ordering UserCF $<$ ItemCF $<$ SVD holds, with the neural
        band (0.285--0.371 HR@10) sitting above SVD.
  \item BPR-MF and NeuMF are within one combined standard deviation of each
        other.
  \item VBPR's HR@10 edge over MultiModalNCF (0.0055) is smaller than one
        MultiModalNCF standard deviation, while MultiModalNCF leads both NDCG
        columns---the pair is read as statistically tied.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Validation}
Table~\ref{tab:cross} compares performance across both datasets, and
Table~\ref{tab:amazon-ablation} repeats the modality ablation on Amazon.

What transfers: the ladder ordering, the BPR-MF~$\approx$~NeuMF tie, the
``single-modality deltas are inside seed noise'' conclusion (on Amazon removing
any single modality moves HR@10 by at most 0.0030, still inside the 0.0085
full-model spread), and the cold-start direction---the content model reaches
HR@10 0.2364$\pm$0.0202 against 0.1204$\pm$0.0120 for the content-blind model and
0.1092 for random, a content/blind ratio of 1.96$\times$ (1.17$\times$ on
MovieLens). What does not: on Amazon the top HR@10 point estimate belongs to
LightGCN (0.1187 vs 0.1164), a difference well inside the combined spread, and
Behaviour-only~$\approx$~Full (0.1163 vs 0.1164).

  \item \textbf{CI pipeline}: Three jobs run on every push to main and every

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\subsection{Key Findings}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Model ordering is largely consistent across domains.} The
        ladder backbone (UserCF $<$ ItemCF $<$ SVD) and the neural band's lead
        over SVD hold on both MovieLens and Amazon, suggesting these patterns
        are not dataset-specific artifacts. Which model leads \emph{within} the
        neural band is domain-dependent, however (VBPR/MultiModalNCF on
        MovieLens, LightGCN on Amazon), so no cross-domain ``best model'' is
        claimed.
  \item \textbf{Content features help in cold-start, but not much in overall
        ranking.} Modality ablation shows that removing content features has
        limited impact on overall ranking quality, but cold-start experiments
        show measurable gains when content is available.
  \item \textbf{MultiModalNCF is competitive with established baselines.}
        Despite using a simple concatenation-based fusion, MultiModalNCF
        performs on par with VBPR, a well-established content-aware baseline.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single split.} We use one leave-last-5-out partition by design;
        conclusions are read against seed spread, not split variance. No
        cross-validation is performed.
  \item \textbf{No significance testing.} Differences are compared against
        reported standard deviations; formal statistical tests are not run.
        Small deltas (e.g., VBPR vs MultiModalNCF HR@10) are explicitly left
        undecided.
  \item \textbf{Epoch budgets are not equalised.} MultiModalNCF runs 10 epochs
        against VBPR's 30 and BPR-MF/LightGCN's 50, so accuracy comparisons
        carry that asymmetry.
  \item \textbf{Training-time measurement conditions.} Hardware, warm/cold
        cache conditions are not recorded alongside reported seconds.
  \item \textbf{Second-domain slice represents heavy users only.} The Amazon
        slice is restricted to users with $\geq20$ ratings, chosen to match the
        MovieLens population rule, and does not describe the full Amazon
        population.
  \item \textbf{Content ablation effect is small.} Removing all content costs
        about one seed std on MovieLens and nothing measurable on Amazon.
  \item \textbf{Free-tier deployments sleep when idle.} Reported live behaviour
        may include cold-start latency on first request.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We presented CineRec, a practical comparative study of six recommendation
models across two domains, with two published baselines and multi-seed
validation. Our findings confirm that (1) model rankings are largely
consistent across datasets, (2) content features provide measurable benefits in
cold-start scenarios, and (3) a simple multi-modal extension of NeuMF can
compete with established content-aware baselines like VBPR.

Future work includes: (1) evaluating on larger-scale datasets such as KuaiRec
or Yelp, (2) exploring more sophisticated fusion strategies beyond simple
concatenation, (3) conducting formal significance testing, and (4) extending the
cold-start study to user cold-start as well as item cold-start. All code and
evaluation scripts are available at:
\url{https://github.com/ElijahZhao/cinerec}
